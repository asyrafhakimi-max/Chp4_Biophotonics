What is biophotonics?

The big picture before we start.

0.1

Light as a medical tool

In plain words

Biophotonics is about using light to see inside the body, to measure what it is made of, and to move or treat cells. A pulse oximeter on your finger, an eye scan at the optician and a laser that corrects eyesight are all biophotonics.

Lecture detail: three definitions

1. The science of generating and harnessing light (photons) to image, detect and manipulate biological materials (T. Huser, IEEE-LEOS, 2004).

2. An interdisciplinary science studying the interaction of light with biological material, where “light” includes all radiant energy whose quantum unit is the photon (D. Matthews, 2007).

3. The application of light and other forms of radiant energy to the life sciences.

Biophotonics is a two-way street: photonics for biomedicine (this lecture) and biomaterials for photonics (biological molecules used as optical materials for lasers, data storage and signal processing).

0.2

Four ways it helps health care

In plain words

Light either reads information out of the body (imaging and diagnosis) or puts energy in (therapy). Engineers design the instruments for both.

Reads information

Fluorescence and confocal microscopes, OCT scans of the retina, tracking single molecules.

Reads information

01

The nature of light

What light is, how its energy is counted, and why lasers behave differently from lamps.

1.1

Colour, wavelength and photon energy

In plain words

Light travels as a wave. The distance from one wave crest to the next is the wavelength (λ), measured in nanometres (nm). Our eyes see only 400–700 nm. Shorter waves (ultraviolet, UV) carry more energy in each packet of light; longer waves (infrared, IR) carry less. Medicine mostly uses UV, visible and near-infrared light.

E = hc / λ ≈ 1240 / λ(nm) eVShorter wavelength → more energy per photon.

1.2

A wave and a stream of photons

In plain words

Light behaves like ripples on a pond (a wave) and also like raindrops (separate packets of energy called photons). Both pictures are true. We use the wave picture to explain focusing and blurring in microscopes, and the photon picture to explain how molecules soak up light.

Explains interference, diffraction and polarisation → microscope resolution, OCT.

Explains absorption, emission and photodetectors → spectroscopy, optical tweezers.

Lecture detail

As a wave: oscillating electric (E) and magnetic (B) fields, perpendicular to each other and to the direction of travel. c = 3.00 × 10⁸ m/s in vacuum; in a medium v = c/n (tissue n ≈ 1.35–1.55).

As photons: energy arrives in discrete packets. E = hν = hc/λ, momentum p = h/λ, Planck’s constant h = 6.626 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s.

1.3

Coherence: waves marching in step

In plain words

Coherent light is like soldiers marching in step: all the waves rise and fall together. A laser does this, so it can be focused to a tiny spot. A lamp is like a crowd walking in all directions: its waves are out of step. Surprisingly, light that is only briefly in step is exactly what OCT eye scanners need.

L ≈ λ² / ΔλCoherence length: how far the waves stay in step. A narrow colour range (small Δλ) gives a long L.

The laser stays in step for about 20 cm; the LED for only about 20 µm. OCT uses that short distance on purpose to slice tissue into thin depth layers (see 4.7).

Lecture detail: sources and the worked example

Temporal coherence is set by the colour spread: one frequency (narrow Δλ) stays in phase along the beam. Spatial coherence is a constant phase across the beam, so it stays narrow and focuses to a small spot.

1.4

Matter can act like a wave too

In plain words

Tiny particles such as electrons also behave like waves. A fast electron has a wavelength thousands of times shorter than light. That is why an electron microscope can see far smaller details than a light microscope. Molecules also hold energy only in fixed “steps”, which is why each one absorbs only certain colours.

λ = h / mvde Broglie wavelength. For an electron: λ ≈ 1.226 / √V nm.

Light–tissue interaction

2.1

Five things light can do in tissue

In plain words

When light hits skin it can bounce off (reflection), bend (refraction), be soaked up (absorption), bounce around inside (scattering) or pass straight through (transmission). Every optical medical device measures some mix of these five.

2.2

Light-eating molecules and the optical window

In plain words

Tissue contains molecules that soak up light, called chromophores. Blood (haemoglobin) and skin pigment (melanin) soak up blue and green light. Water soaks up infrared beyond about 1350 nm. Between about 650 and 1350 nm very little is absorbed, so light travels deepest. This gap is the optical window, and it is why a torch makes your fingertip glow red.

2.3

Beer–Lambert law: more stuff, less light

In plain words

Think of tea: the stronger the tea, or the wider the cup, the less light gets through. Absorbance (A) grows in a straight line with the concentration (c) and the path length (l). So if you measure A, you can work out how much of a substance is in the sample.

A = log₁₀(I₀/I) = ε c lI₀ = light in, I = light out, ε = how strongly the molecule absorbs at this wavelength.

Simulation · Virtual spectrophotometer cuvette

Absorbance A0.311
Light through, T48.9 %
Calculation6220 × 50 µM × 1 cm

Try it

  1. Set the concentration to 0. Every photon reaches the detector (T = 100 %, A = 0).
  2. Set it to 25 µM, then 50 µM. A doubles exactly (0.156 → 0.311): the graph is a straight line.
  3. Keep 50 µM and change the path length to 2 cm. A doubles again.
  4. Switch to Protein (BSA). It absorbs about 7 times more strongly, so very little light gets through.

What to notice

A = 1 means only 10 % of the light gets through; A = 2 means 1 %. Clinical analysers measure NADH at 340 nm this way to run enzyme tests such as LDH and ALT.

Lecture detail: symbols and the worked example

A = absorbance (no unit); I₀, I = incident and transmitted intensity; T = I/I₀, so A = −log₁₀T; ε = molar absorptivity (L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹), depends on λ; c = concentration (mol L⁻¹); l = path length (cm).

Worked example. NADH in a 1 cm cuvette reads A = 0.311 at 340 nm; ε₃₄₀ = 6220 L mol⁻¹ cm⁻¹. (a) c = A/(εl) = 0.311/(6220 × 1) = 5.0 × 10⁻⁵ mol L⁻¹ = 50 µM. (b) T = 10^(−0.311) = 0.489 → 48.9 % transmitted.

In tissue, scattering removes light too: I = I₀ e^(−μt z), with μt = μa + μs. Plain Beer–Lambert holds only for clear, non-scattering samples.

2.4

Scattering: light playing pinball

In plain words

Scattering is light bouncing off tiny structures and changing direction. Very small particles scatter blue light much more than red (that is why the sky is blue) and send light in all directions. Particles about the size of the wavelength, like cell nuclei and mitochondria, scatter mostly forwards. In tissue, scattering usually limits how deep light can go.

2.5

What absorbed light does to tissue

In plain words

Absorbed light energy has to go somewhere. It can come back out as a new colour (fluorescence), start a chemical reaction, heat the tissue, or, with very short and intense pulses, tear molecules apart. Doctors choose the effect by choosing the wavelength, the power and how long each pulse lasts. Chapter 3 covers this in detail.

03

Spectroscopy

Splitting light into its wavelengths to find out what a sample contains.

3.1

Absorption, emission and fluorescence

In plain words

A molecule can only hold certain amounts of energy, like steps on a staircase. It absorbs a photon only if the photon’s energy matches a step. It then loses a little energy as heat and drops back down, sending out a new photon. The new photon has a little less energy, so its colour shifts towards red: blue in, green out. This is fluorescence, and the colour shift is the Stokes shift.

ΔE = hνA photon is absorbed only when its energy equals the gap between two energy levels.

Try it

  1. Tap Shine blue light. Watch the electron jump up from S₀ to S₁.
  2. See it slide down a little (energy lost as heat), then fall back and send out a green photon.
  3. Tick Allow the rare slow path and run it a few times. Sometimes the electron crosses to T₁ and glows orange much later: phosphorescence, the “glow in the dark”.

What to notice

The out-going light always has a longer wavelength than the light coming in. Microscopes use this colour difference to block the blue light and keep only the glow.

Lecture detail: ways to reach the excited state and timescales

Absorbing a photon → photoluminescence (fluorescence, phosphorescence). A chemical reaction → chemiluminescence (e.g. luciferase bioluminescence assays). Heat → thermal emission (flame emission for Na⁺ and K⁺).

Inside a spectrophotometer

In plain words

A spectrophotometer shines one colour at a time through a sample and measures how much light gets through. Plotting absorbance against wavelength gives a spectrum: a fingerprint. The position of the peak tells you what is in the tube; with Beer–Lambert, the height tells you how much.

3.3

Clinical example: the pulse oximeter

In plain words

The clip on your finger shines red (660 nm) and infrared (940 nm) light through it. Blood full of oxygen absorbs more infrared; blood low in oxygen absorbs more red. With every heartbeat a little more blood squeezes into the finger, so the signal pulses. The device compares the red and infrared pulses to estimate the oxygen saturation (SpO₂).

Try it

  1. Watch the two LEDs take turns (slowed right down; real devices switch hundreds of times a second).
  2. Lower Oxygen in the blood to 85 %. The red pulse grows bigger than the infrared one, and R goes up.
  3. Change the heart rate. The pulses come faster or slower, but the SpO₂ reading does not change.

What to notice

Dividing by the pulsing part cancels skin, bone and veins, leaving only arterial blood. Normal SpO₂ is about 95–100 %. The simulation uses the textbook approximation SpO₂ ≈ 110 − 25R; real devices use their own calibration curve.

Lecture detail

1. Two LEDs flash in turn: red (660 nm) and infrared (940 nm). 2. Deoxy-Hb absorbs more at 660 nm; oxy-Hb absorbs more at 940 nm. 3. A photodiode measures transmitted light; pulsing arterial blood adds a small AC signal on a large DC level. 4. Ratio of ratios R = (AC₆₆₀/DC₆₆₀) ÷ (AC₉₄₀/DC₉₄₀) → SpO₂ from a calibration curve.

One small device combines the optical window (2.2), Beer–Lambert (2.3) and spectroscopy at two wavelengths. Accuracy drops with poor circulation, movement, carbon monoxide poisoning and, for some devices, darker skin.

3.5

Raman: a fingerprint without a label

In plain words

Almost all scattered light keeps its colour. But about one photon in a million or more comes back with a slightly different colour, because it gave a little energy to a vibrating chemical bond. Each kind of bond shifts the colour by its own amount, so these rare photons form a chemical fingerprint. No dye is needed, so living cells can be checked as they are.

Δν̃ = 1/λ₀ − 1/λₛRaman shift in cm⁻¹: laser wavelength λ₀, scattered wavelength λₛ.

Optical bioimaging

From single molecules to the human retina: seeing biology with light.

4.1

Why image with light?

In plain words

X-ray, CT, MRI and ultrasound each have limits: radiation dose, little chemical information, or not enough detail. Light is safe, can see details down to single molecules and can report chemistry. Its weakness: it cannot go deep, only millimetres to a few centimetres. So light is used where it can reach: skin, eyes, inside endoscopes, during surgery, and in cell samples.

4.2

How small can a light microscope see?

In plain words

Because light is a wave, even a perfect microscope blurs each tiny point into a small fuzzy spot, about 200 nm wide. If two points are closer than that, they merge into one blob. You can sharpen the image with a shorter wavelength or a better lens (higher numerical aperture, NA), or by using a super-resolution trick such as STED.

d = λ / (2 NA)Abbe limit (1873). Green light, oil lens NA 1.4: d ≈ 180 nm.

4.3

Seeing transparent cells without stain

In plain words

Most cells are almost transparent, like a glass bead in water. A normal (bright-field) microscope barely sees them unless they are stained, which kills them. Phase contrast and DIC turn the tiny delay light picks up inside a cell into brightness, so living, unstained cells become visible. Polarised light makes ordered structures such as collagen, or gout crystals, glow.

Simulation · Switch the microscope mode

Bright-field: the living cells are almost invisible.

Try it

  1. Start in Bright-field: can you even find the cells?
  2. Tap Phase contrast, then DIC. The same living cells now show edges and nuclei.
  3. Tap Stained: very clear, but the cells had to be killed and fixed first.
  4. Tap Polarised light: only the needle-shaped crystals glow, which is how gout is diagnosed from joint fluid.
Lecture detail

Phase contrast (Zernike, 1932; Nobel Prize 1953) converts phase delays from refractive-index differences into brightness. DIC (Nomarski) interferes two slightly shifted, polarised beams and shows phase gradients as a pseudo-3D relief. Polarisation microscopy with crossed polarisers reveals birefringent structures: collagen, muscle fibres, amyloid, urate crystals, asbestos fibres. Example from the lecture: live HeLa cells, invisible in bright-field, show nuclei and edges clearly under phase contrast and DIC.

4.4

The fluorescence microscope

In plain words

Blue light is shone onto a sample that has green-glowing labels. A special mirror called a dichroic reflects the blue light down onto the sample but lets the green glow pass up to the camera. Filters remove any leftover blue, so only the labelled parts shine against a black background.

Blue goes down, green comes up. This only works because of the Stokes shift (3.1).

Variant

TIRF

Lights up only about 100 nm above the glass, ideal for watching events at the cell membrane.

Variant

FRET

Two dyes swap energy only when 1–10 nm apart: a “molecular ruler” to see if two proteins touch.

4.5

Sharp slices: confocal and two-photon

In plain words

In a thick sample, glow from above and below the focus makes the picture hazy. A confocal microscope puts a pinhole in front of the detector to block that out-of-focus glow. A two-photon microscope makes only the focus glow in the first place, using near-infrared light that also reaches deeper into tissue.

Lecture detail

Confocal (Marvin Minsky, patent 1957): a laser lights one point; a pinhole at the matching (confocal) image plane passes only light from the focal plane. Scanning mirrors build the image point by point; stacking slices gives 3D. Trade-offs: slower, more photobleaching, the pinhole throws signal away.

Two-photon (Denk, Strickler and Webb, 1990): two near-IR photons absorbed together supply the energy of one visible photon (2 × 800 nm ≈ one 400 nm photon). Probability ∝ I², so only the focal volume fluoresces. Near-IR scatters less, imaging ≈ 0.5–1 mm deep. Needs a femtosecond pulsed laser (e.g. Ti:sapphire, ≈ 100 fs, 80 MHz).

In plain words

Eric Betzig, Stefan Hell and William Moerner won the 2014 Nobel Prize in Chemistry for this. Try the STED switch in the 4.2 simulation.

4.7

Clinical example: OCT eye scan

In plain words

OCT is like ultrasound, but with light. Light is split into two paths: one goes to a moving mirror, the other into the eye. The two beams only add up (interfere) when both paths are the same length, to within the short coherence length (1.3). By moving the mirror, the machine reads the echo from each depth and builds a picture of the retina’s layers, with micrometre detail.

5.1

Optical tweezers: holding cells with light

In plain words

Light gives a tiny push. When a laser is focused very tightly, a small bead or cell is pulled towards the brightest spot, the focus, and held there like a marble in a bowl. The force is tiny, a few piconewtons (pN), but just right to hold a cell or to measure the pull of a single protein motor. Arthur Ashkin invented it in 1986 (Nobel Prize in Physics, 2018).

F = Q nₘ W / cQ = trapping efficiency, nₘ = refractive index of water (1.33), W = laser power, c = speed of light.

Lecture detail: physics and the worked example

Photons carry momentum. When a bead bends (refracts) the light, the light’s momentum changes, so the bead feels an equal and opposite force. The gradient force pulls a particle with higher refractive index than water towards the brightest point; the scattering force pushes it along the beam. A stable 3D trap needs a high-NA objective (NA > 1).

Worked example. Polystyrene bead in water, nₘ = 1.33, W = 10 mW, Q = 0.1: F = (0.1 × 1.33 × 0.010)/(3.00 × 10⁸) = 4.4 × 10⁻¹² N = 4.4 pN.

What optical tweezers are used for

Move cells without touching

Pick up, position and sort single cells inside a sealed, sterile chamber.

Measure molecular motors

Forces (pN) and steps (nm) of kinesin, myosin and RNA polymerase.

Measure mechanics

Stretch DNA; test how stretchy red blood cells are.

Raman tweezers

Hold one cell in the beam and read its Raman fingerprint (3.5).

Strengths: no contact, works in normal buffer, forces of 0.1–100 pN and movements of 1–500 nm match molecular motors. Limitations: light can damage or heat the sample (reduced with 1064 nm); only small forces.

5.3

Laser nanosurgery inside living cells

In plain words

Very short, very intense laser pulses (femtoseconds) are focused inside a cell. Only at the focus is the light strong enough to vaporise material, so scientists can cut a single fibre inside a living cell without breaking the cell open. It is the same physics as two-photon microscopy, with more energy.

Ablation

Destroy one organelle (a mitochondrion, centrosome or nerve branch) and watch the cell respond.

Dissection

Cut single microtubules or parts of chromosomes to study forces and repair.

Induction

Disturb the cell’s skeleton or flow on purpose, then watch it recover.

Femtosecond pulses need about 100× less energy than nanosecond pulses to make a plasma, so there is less heat and shock damage (Vogel et al., 2005).

Review

Key takeaways and the words to know.

  • 01Light is a wave (λ, coherence) and a stream of photons (E = hc/λ). The energy of each photon decides what light can do.
  • 02Absorption (Beer–Lambert) and scattering limit how deep light goes; 650–1350 nm is the optical window.
  • 03Spectroscopy reads chemistry: absorption for amount, fluorescence for labelled targets, Raman for label-free fingerprints.
  • 04Optical imaging trades depth for detail: 20 nm super-resolution to millimetre-deep OCT.
  • 05Light carries momentum: focused lasers can hold, measure and cut single cells.

Glossary

Wavelength (λ)
Distance between two wave crests; sets the colour of light.
Photon
One packet of light energy, E = hc/λ.
Coherence
How well light waves stay in step with each other.
Chromophore
A molecule that absorbs light, e.g. haemoglobin, melanin, water.
Optical window
650–1350 nm, where tissue absorbs least and light goes deepest.
Absorbance (A)
log₁₀(I₀/I); grows with concentration and path length.
Scattering
Light changing direction after hitting small structures.
Fluorescence
Light given out after absorbing light, at a longer wavelength.
Stokes shift
The colour difference between absorbed and emitted light.
Raman scattering
Rare scattering that changes colour by a bond’s vibration energy.
Numerical aperture (NA)
How much light a lens collects; higher NA = finer detail.
Confocal pinhole
A tiny hole that blocks out-of-focus light.
OCT
Optical coherence tomography: depth imaging by interference, “ultrasound with light”.
Piconewton (pN)
10⁻¹² N, the force scale of molecular motors and optical traps.
